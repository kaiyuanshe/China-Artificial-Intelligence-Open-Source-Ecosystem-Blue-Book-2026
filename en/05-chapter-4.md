**Chapter 4: Practical problems in the development of China's AI open source ecosystem**

**4.1 Insufficient understanding of ecosystem attributes, a relatively strong industrial mindset**

**4.1.1 Government: a weak grasp of ecosystem attributes, a weak sense of role boundaries**

An AI open source ecosystem is in essence a self-organizing, self-evolving mechanism of social collaboration. Cultivating its internal mechanisms — technical consensus, developer networks, community self-governance — depends on stable rules, a tolerant environment and the accumulation of time. The past approach of advancing industrial projects with an emphasis on the number of projects and platform carriers may suppress the vitality of multiple actors and makes it hard to build long-term trust.

Advancing AI+ projects in industrial-project style also tends to leave public infrastructure undersupplied and standards unbuilt. The absence of key support such as affordable computing power, public testing environments and open source community governance platforms can leave the innovation base of smaller actors unprotected; and in areas such as open source compliance, intellectual property protection and data security, the lack of unified rule guidance and regulatory mechanisms, with excessive reliance on market participants to build things on their own, can leave companies afraid to take part boldly in open source practice for fear of infringement risk. The eventual result may be a fragmented and divided AI open source ecosystem\[56\].

The traditional industrial mindset is also visible in investment promotion, which focuses excessively on attracting large enterprises: it shows insufficient understanding of asset-light innovators such as OPCs and offers little targeted support, so many technically capable individual innovators can earn income in the open source ecosystem but find it hard to obtain stable expectations and institutional guarantees. The open source ecosystem's function as a "buffer" and a "diffuser" of employment has not been fully released. In the longer term this may also weaken a region's employment resilience and capacity for structural transformation in the new round of intelligent transformation.

**4.1.2 Enterprises: short-sighted strategic positioning, a relatively weak ecosystem mindset**

In building open source ecosystems, enterprises easily fall into two traps: "wait-and-see-ism" and "short-termism". Some companies believe that joining open source while AI technology is evolving rapidly will not produce controllable returns and will instead disperse resources and expose their capabilities, so they cut exploratory R&D spending and choose to wait until technology routes, engineering systems and application models become clearer before following. In the short term this strategy does help reduce the cost of exploring any single project, but in the long run it tends to leave companies permanently in the position of technology follower and capability borrower. Other companies treat open source merely as a traffic entry point or a tool for commercial monetization, measuring its value by short-term commercial returns; their open source investment lacks patience and their spending on foundational technology is insufficient, so they find it hard to accumulate the real value of the open source ecosystem and drift toward the margins in ecosystem competition.

At the same time, some companies have an insufficient understanding of the core trait of the open source ecosystem — collaboration among multiple actors and bottom-up evolution — and overemphasize their own control over and leadership of projects, tending to treat open source projects as an extension of their in-house R&D rather than a public collaboration platform. This single-actor-centered approach to open source objectively suppresses the deep participation of external developers, researchers and industry partners, weakening the unique advantages of open source in expanding the space for exploration and bringing together diverse innovation paths**.**

**4.1.3 Universities: one-dimensional evaluation criteria, seriously lagging capability development**

In recent years the Chinese government has issued a number of policies to advance AI open source education and talent development, forming an initial policy direction that moves "from encouragement to institutionalized support". In the *Opinions on Deeply Implementing the "AI Plus" Initiative* issued by the State Council in 2025, for example, it explicitly states: "establish and improve evaluation and incentive mechanisms for contributions to AI open source; encourage universities to include open source contributions in student credit recognition and faculty achievement assessment; support universities, enterprises and research institutions in exploring affordable and efficient open source application models; and accelerate the building of an internationally influential open source technology system and community ecosystem."\[57\]

In practice, however, **there is still the pain point of uneven progress in implementation and an incomplete evaluation system**. Within most universities, open source contributions still carry very limited weight in professional title review and credit recognition, and unified standards for specific evaluation indicators, evaluation processes and incentive mechanisms have yet to take shape. A considerable number of faculty members and students lack a clear understanding of how open source contributions can be turned into assessable results, so open source activity is still hard to convert directly into hard criteria for career development or academic progress.

**At the same time, China's university system for developing open source capabilities is still at an early stage.** Domestic AI education currently focuses on basic awareness, tool-use training and curriculum development, and some leading institutions have already brought open source projects into the classroom as platforms for engineering practice and capability building, which is itself important progress. Open source capability, however, goes beyond technical "drills": it also includes a working habit of transparent collaboration, an awareness of contributing upstream, respect for rules and the responsibility of long-term maintenance. The talent an open source ecosystem needs is not only a research-oriented or tool-oriented engineer but a "community-oriented technical leader" who can contribute continuously in a community, collaborate across organizations and understand the logic of governance. If universities stop at technical training and do not include open source culture, collaboration ethics and a sense of global participation in a systematic development framework, the supply of talent will struggle to support the long-term prosperity of the AI open source ecosystem.

Finally, universities still aim their talent development primarily at employment in research institutions or large enterprises, paying insufficient attention to distributed forms of innovative employment such as OPCs. As micro production units, OPCs require individuals to have not only engineering implementation skills but also use-case understanding, business thinking and the ability to collaborate with multiple parties. Disciplinary walls within universities remain high and paths for developing such all-round qualities have not yet entered the mainstream teaching system. This mismatch between the structure of talent development and the forms of employment makes it hard for the talent universities supply to match the needs of distributed innovation and OPC development within the AI open source ecosystem.

**4.2 Poor industrial connectivity, blocked technology adoption**

**There is a disconnect between the structure of technology supply and local industry demand.** Domestic open source AI supply is currently concentrated in general-purpose large models, foundational frameworks and lower-level toolchains, whereas local industry most urgently needs engineered deployment and solutions within industry use cases. Between the two there is no mature "middle layer" capability to connect them, such as industry-adaptation components, standardized toolkits and reusable systems integration solutions. This structural disconnect leaves companies facing the predicament of "usable but not pleasant to use, runnable but hard to deploy" in practice: high deployment costs and long tuning cycles raise the barrier to adoption. Some leading large model companies deliver industry models and complete solutions as closed source; although this is efficient for delivery in specific use cases, it is often expensive and, constrained by resource bottlenecks, can cover only some industries and leading enterprises. Small and medium-sized enterprises find it hard to bear deployment costs continuously, to build independent iteration capability, or to reach all sectors of the economy. An open source middle layer can both lower the deployment threshold and preserve room for secondary development, but it is difficult to build and requires long-term community accumulation and industrial collaboration. Platforms in China such as the OpenI community and ModelScope are attempting to build model tooling and industry adaptation capabilities, but they still face practical constraints including insufficient sustained investment, limited industry participation and immature business models, and the middle-layer ecosystem has yet to reach a stable scale.

**Local enterprises have relatively weak absorptive capacity.** Many small and medium-sized enterprises have limited reserves of AI talent and lack both systems integration capability and teams able to carry out deep secondary development and long-term maintenance of open source projects; they can often only call models, not adapt them. Without sustained technical investment and community participation, open source capability can hardly accumulate into real corporate competitiveness, and technology deployment remains at the pilot stage, hard to replicate at scale.

**Insufficient coordination of key elements raises deployment costs.** Local use-case data is generally fragmented and standards are inconsistent, and cross-departmental flows are restricted; there is also a structural mismatch between the layout of computing resources and real application demand. Where data, computing power and use cases are not systematically coordinated, even advanced underlying models are hard to convert into stable and sustainable business value.

**The open source ecosystem has not yet been fully incorporated into the overall framework of local industry-chain upgrading.** When releasing "lists of open application use cases", some regions focus more on bringing in technology and projects in list form to advance isolated applications, paying insufficient attention to how technology can keep evolving around the nodes of the local leading industry chain and how a long-term multi-stakeholder collaboration mechanism can be formed. The open source ecosystem is therefore easily seen as an investment promotion or project tool rather than long-term capacity building embedded in the structure of the industry chain. China has already explored computing platforms and industry large model pilots, but institutional arrangements for organizing open source collaborative innovation around core industry-chain enterprises remain limited, and no stable structure has yet formed along the lines of long-term evolution around key technology nodes. This pattern of "project-based introduction" rather than "chain-based construction" makes technology deployment episodic and hard to accumulate into sustainable industrial capability.

**4.3 Insufficient innovation conversion, insufficient job creation**

**Uncertainty in open source intellectual property rules affects the conversion and deployment of open source results.** In current AI-related open source practice in China, there is still considerable ambiguity about the boundaries of open source license application, copyright and compliance responsibility for model training data, and the definition of rights and obligations in the secondary development and distribution of open source technology. Companies and developers therefore face the practical constraints of high compliance costs and hard-to-assess infringement risk, and tend to reduce the depth of their open source work and invest conversion resources cautiously. Even where technology supply and industry demand are matched, they may be unwilling or afraid to convert it because of property and compliance risks\[58\].

**There is a structural disconnect between the level of activity in the open source ecosystem and the ability to convert it commercially.** The number of domestic AI open source projects is growing quickly, but few have formed sustainable business models and long-term community operation capability. This was already a prominent problem in the software open source era. Research by Tsinghua University shows that China's input–output efficiency in foundational software is about 1:1.77, lower than the United States at about 1:4.17\[59\], reflecting a still-weak chain from technology supply to commercial return. Limited commercialization makes it hard to convert open source results steadily into sustainable business forms and jobs.

**The value of ecosystem-wide diffusion by innovators in creating jobs has yet to appear.** New kinds of innovators, such as super individuals and OPCs, keep emerging, but their innovation activities are mostly concentrated in vertical use-case plug-ins, tool component development and technology packaging optimization; their service forms are dispersed and they work very much as individuals. On the one hand, the commercial collaboration networks formed around open source projects are still at an exploratory stage. Most OPCs still compete in the market by providing individualized services, and no large-scale division-of-labor network of "OPC + small and medium-sized enterprises" or "OPC + community collaboration" has formed. On the other hand, the existing systems for investment and financing, industrial support and performance evaluation are still designed mainly around the scale and fixed-asset investment of traditional enterprises, and fit asset-light, code-driven and distributed forms of innovation only to a limited degree. All of this constrains the multiplier effect that innovation and entrepreneurship could release into regional economies.

**An information gap and a cognitive barrier remain between innovation and entrepreneurship and open source technology.** Some local innovators, especially small and medium-sized enterprises and startup teams from traditional industries, do not sufficiently understand the accessibility of open source resources, the paths for using them and the boundaries of compliance, and fail to fully incorporate open source models, frameworks and toolchains into their innovation plans. At the same time, there are no effective channels connecting open source communities with local industries, and information asymmetry between technology supply and entrepreneurial demand reduces the ability of open source technology to support new business forms and new jobs.

**4.4 Lagging ecosystem development, a co-governance structure still to be cultivated**

China's AI open source ecosystem is currently dominated by the center-led model, with strong resource integration and project incubation capabilities, but community-based collaboration and long-term contribution mechanisms are still being explored. Ecosystem development focuses on visible carriers such as platform building, project initiation and resource integration, and relatively neglects indicators of ecosystem prosperity and stability such as community operations and contribution relationships, which easily produces the result of "platforms and projects, but no community".

Collaboration among enterprises, universities and developers currently takes project-based cooperation as its main form, with clear short-term goals, but mechanisms for long-term co-evolution across parties, sustained arrangements for coordinating interests and the building of independent governance structures are still being explored and refined. Relatively limited international collaboration capability also makes it hard for domestic projects to embed themselves in global contribution networks, further weakening the ecosystem's external linkages. The result is short project lifecycles, a limited number of core contributors, an unstable pace of technological evolution, and an ecosystem whose self-organizing and self-repairing capabilities are still being cultivated.

**4.5 Insufficient self-reliance and controllability, a lacking foundational system**

China's AI technology system does not yet cover the full stack; its foundational infrastructure in particular is underdeveloped and the talent gap is large, and it is still at the stage of following US standards. Moreover, self-reliance and controllability mean not only technological substitutability but also a voice in the global open source governance structure. Without deep participation in rule-making in international communities, self-reliance and controllability will remain confined to a closed domestic system.

Building foundational AI infrastructure projects requires a highly error-tolerant innovation incentive mechanism and patient investment, as well as neutral rule guidance that keeps pace with the speed of expansion; China is currently at a stage of insufficient AI open source projects and an absence of third-party neutral organizations. This situation reflects China's current catch-up and application-oriented stage of development, but it also means that "moving from projects to an ecosystem" and "moving from isolated successes to long-term sustainability" lack sufficient institutional support. As AI technology moves from usable to general-purpose and expands from single use cases to cross-industry collaboration, patient capital and governance capability need to shift from optional items to foundational capabilities.
